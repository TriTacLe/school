# Solutions, exams 2022 and 2024

Worked answers for the four exam papers in `past-papers/`: the full 2022 paper (180 marks, Abiodun), the 2024 Section C multiple choice, the 2024 thermodynamics section, and the 2024 supplementary Section C. None of them ship a memo.

Read the three MCQ sets side by side. Roughly a third of the 2024 supp questions are the 2024 main paper reworded, usually with one condition flipped, and a dozen more come straight out of the 2022 paper. The flips are the point: **super**-geostrophic becomes **sub**-geostrophic, **12** hours becomes **48** hours, gas **to** solid becomes solid **to** gas, **is** correct becomes is **not** correct. Read the stem twice before the options.

## Exam 2024, thermodynamics section (30 marks)

### Question 1, the two soundings, Figure 1 (8 marks)

**1.1 The night-time panel is B (3 marks).** B has a surface-based inversion: the temperature climbs from about 5 C at the ground to about 18.5 C at 600 m. That is what radiative cooling of the ground under clear skies does overnight, and it is strongest in Northern Cape winter, when nights are long and the air is dry. Panel A has no such feature. Its lowest 700 m falls at close to the dry adiabatic rate, which is the signature of a convectively mixed layer driven by daytime surface heating, and its inversion is elevated instead of surface-based.

**1.2 The day-time profile is A. The entrainment-zone inversion runs from about 1000 m to about 1500 m (2 marks).** Read off where the black curve stops cooling with height and starts warming (about 1000 m, roughly 12 C) and where it reaches its warm peak (about 1500 m, roughly 17.5 C).

### Question 3, moist thermodynamics (15 marks)

**3.1 Classification (8 marks).** The whole question is one comparison: place Gamma_e against Gamma_s = 6 K/km and Gamma_d = 10 K/km.

| | Layer | Classification | Reason |
| - | ----- | -------------- | ------ |
| (a) | Gamma_e = 0.005 K/m | Stable | 5 K/km sits below Gamma_s, so neither a dry nor a saturated parcel keeps rising. |
| (b) | 26 C to 14 C over 1000 m | Absolutely unstable | 12 K/km is steeper than Gamma_d. |
| (c) | 1 km deep inversion | Stable | Gamma_e is negative, so it is below Gamma_s by a wide margin. |
| (d) | Shallow foggy inversion | Stable | Saturation does not rescue it. Gamma_e is still negative, still below Gamma_s. The fog is a distractor. |
| (e) | Thoroughly mixed unsaturated layer over an intensely heated dark surface | Neutrally stable with respect to a dry air parcel | Thorough dry mixing is what makes Gamma_e relax to Gamma_d. |
| (f) | Theta_eq constant, 12 percent RH throughout | Stable | Constant Theta_eq means Gamma_e = Gamma_s = 6 K/km. At 12 percent RH nothing will ever saturate, so the only parcel that matters is a dry one, and it cools at 10 K/km against an environment cooling at 6. It sinks back. |
| (g) | Theta_eq constant, 100 percent RH throughout | Neutrally stable with respect to a saturated air parcel | Same lapse rate as (f), but now the parcel really is saturated, so it cools at Gamma_s too and neither gains nor loses buoyancy. |
| (h) | Gamma_e between 0.008 and 0.009 K/m | Conditionally unstable | 8 to 9 K/km lies between Gamma_s and Gamma_d across the whole range. |

The pairing of (f) and (g) is the examiner's point. Identical lapse rates, different answers, because the label neutral only means anything for a parcel that can actually follow that adiabat.

## Exam 2022, Section B (answer any four, 15 marks each)

### Q2 (a) Five processes producing convergence and divergence (10 marks)

Frame every one against the continuity equation: horizontal convergence must be compensated by vertical stretching, and horizontal divergence by shrinking. One diagram per process.

1. **Speed convergence and divergence.** Flow decelerating along its own direction piles mass up. This is the jet-streak entrance and exit mechanism.
2. **Directional (confluent and diffluent) convergence and divergence.** Streamlines coming together or splaying apart at constant speed. The classic case is confluence into a trough.
3. **Frictional convergence and divergence.** In the boundary layer, the cross-isobar component spirals air into a low and out of a high, giving Ekman pumping and suction.
4. **Vorticity-driven convergence and divergence.** Conserving (zeta + f)/D, a column moving into a region of stronger cyclonic vorticity must stretch, which requires low-level convergence. This is what links upper divergence downstream of a trough to surface cyclogenesis.
5. **Thermally and topographically forced convergence and divergence.** Differential heating (sea breeze fronts, heat lows) and forced ascent over terrain both create low-level convergence lines.

### Q2 (b) Divergence around Johannesburg (5 marks)

Convert each report to components first, remembering that a meteorological direction is the direction the wind comes **from**, so u = -|V| sin(psi) and v = -|V| cos(psi).

| Station | Report | u (east) | v (north) |
| ------- | ------ | -------- | --------- |
| 50 km east | 10 m/s from 180 | 0 | +10 |
| 50 km north | 10 m/s from 090 | -10 | 0 |
| 50 km west | 10 m/s from 000 | 0 | -10 |
| 50 km south | 10 m/s from 270 | +10 | 0 |

Div = du/dx + dv/dy, and each pair is 100 km apart.

du/dx = (u_east - u_west) / 1.0e5 = (0 - 0) / 1.0e5 = 0
dv/dy = (v_north - v_south) / 1.0e5 = (0 - 0) / 1.0e5 = 0

**Divergence = 0 s^-1.**

That is the whole point of the question, so say so explicitly. The four winds are pure rotation. Compute the vorticity to prove it and pick up the marks for insight:

zeta = dv/dx - du/dy = (10 - (-10))/1.0e5 - ((-10) - 10)/1.0e5 = 2.0e-4 + 2.0e-4 = **4.0e-4 s^-1**

Positive, so anticlockwise, which in the Southern Hemisphere is **anticyclonic**. A non-divergent anticyclonic eddy: no vertical motion implied, so no forced ascent and no cloud from this pattern.

### Q3 Thermal wind over Cape Town (15 marks)

**(a) What is thermal wind (3 marks).** It is not a wind that blows. It is the vector **difference** between the geostrophic wind at two levels, Vt = Vg(upper) - Vg(lower), and it is the geostrophic wind of the thickness field between them. It runs parallel to the mean isotherms of the layer, with cold air on its right in the Southern Hemisphere and on its left in the Northern. Its existence is exactly equivalent to the atmosphere being baroclinic; in a barotropic atmosphere it is zero.

**(b) Reading Figure 1.** Both panels are 850 hPa, the left one geopotential height and the right one temperature, with Cape Town marked by the green cross at about 18.5 E, 34 S. Everything below is a read-off from a printed contour map, so the marker will accept a range. Show the formula, the substitution and the units, which is where the marks actually sit.

Common to all three parts:

|f| = 2 Omega sin(34) = 2 (7.2722e-5)(0.5592) = 8.13e-5 s^-1

**(i) Geostrophic wind at 850 hPa (4 marks).** Heights rise steeply to the southwest, 1520 through 1570, and fall to the northeast towards the 1490 low over the interior. The 10 m contours near Cape Town are about 1.5 degrees of longitude apart, which at 34 S is roughly 150 km.

|Vg| = (g/|f|)(dZ/dn) = (9.8 / 8.13e-5)(10 / 1.5e5) = 120500 x 6.7e-5 = **about 8 m/s**

Direction: low heights lie to the northeast, and in the SH the geostrophic wind keeps low height on its **right**, so the wind blows towards the northwest, that is a **south-easterly**. The plotted wind arrows around Cape Town point up and to the left, which confirms it. Anything from about 6 to 10 m/s from the southeast should score.

**(ii) Thermal wind, 850 to 500 hPa (4 marks).** The 850 hPa isotherms near Cape Town run roughly west-southwest to east-northeast, with about 11 C at the cross, warmer to the north and colder to the south. Successive 2 K isotherms are about 2.3 degrees of latitude apart, roughly 256 km.

|Vt| = (R/|f|)(dT/dn) ln(P1/P2) = (287 / 8.13e-5)(2 / 2.56e5) ln(850/500)
    = (3.53e6)(7.8e-6)(0.531) = **about 15 m/s**

Direction: cold air is to the south, and in the SH the thermal wind keeps cold on its **right**, so it blows towards the east. **Westerly, about 15 m/s.** That is the expected answer for a mid-latitude station, and getting a strong westerly here is the sanity check that your hemisphere convention is right.

**(iii) Geostrophic wind at 500 hPa (4 marks).** Vg(500) = Vg(850) + Vt, as vectors, not as speeds.

Vg(850), 8 m/s from 135 degrees: u = -8 sin(135) = -5.7, v = -8 cos(135) = +5.7
Vt, 15 m/s westerly: u = +15, v = 0

Sum: u = +9.3, v = +5.7, so |Vg(500)| = sqrt(9.3^2 + 5.7^2) = **about 11 m/s**, blowing towards bearing atan2(9.3, 5.7) = 58 degrees, that is towards the east-northeast, so coming **from about 238 degrees, west-southwesterly**.

The physics to state in one line: the low-level southeasterly is overwhelmed by the thermal wind, so the flow veers with height into the mid-latitude westerlies. That is the thermal wind doing its job.

### Q4 (a) Bergeron-Findeisen (10 marks)

The core is one inequality: over the range roughly -40 to 0 C, the saturation vapour pressure over **ice** is lower than over supercooled **water**. Put ice crystals and supercooled droplets in the same parcel and the air is simultaneously supersaturated with respect to ice and at or below saturation with respect to water. Vapour therefore deposits onto the crystals, which lowers the vapour pressure, which pushes the droplets to evaporate to restore it, which feeds more vapour to the crystals. The ice grows at the direct expense of the liquid.

Why it is fast enough for a thunderstorm:

- It is a **diffusional runaway**, not a chance encounter, so it does not have the slow start that pure condensational growth past about 20 microns suffers from.
- Crystals reach precipitable size in minutes rather than the tens of minutes to hours pure collision-coalescence needs.
- Once they are large enough to fall relative to the droplets, **riming and aggregation** take over and growth accelerates again.
- A deep cumulonimbus keeps the whole mixed-phase layer stocked, because the updraft continually lifts fresh liquid water above the freezing level.
- Falling through the melting level turns the particles into raindrops, so mid-latitude rain is very often melted ice even in summer.

Draw the two saturation curves against temperature with the gap between them shaded, and a second sketch of the crystal growing while the droplet shrinks.

### Q6 Anatomy of a hurricane (15 marks)

**Structure.** Eye (20 to 50 km, subsiding, warm, near-calm, cloud-free), eyewall (the ring of deepest convection where the maximum winds and heaviest rain sit), spiral rainbands feeding in, an inflow layer in the lowest kilometre or so, and an upper-level anticyclonic outflow layer near the tropopause. The whole thing is warm-cored, so the low is deepest at the surface and becomes a high aloft.

**Traces along an east to west cross section through the centre.**

- **Pressure**: a V, falling steadily to a sharp minimum in the eye and rising again symmetrically. This is the cleanest trace.
- **Wind speed**: a double peak. Maxima in each eyewall, a deep minimum in the eye, and a decay outward. On the real Earth the two peaks are not equal, because the storm's translation speed adds on one side and subtracts on the other.
- **Wind direction**: a rapid reversal through the eye, since you cross from one side of the vortex to the other.
- **Temperature**: a warm anomaly peaking in the eye, small at the surface and large, up to about 10 K, in the upper troposphere.

**Ingredients for formation.**

1. SST above about 26.5 C through a deep mixed layer, to supply latent heat
2. Latitude beyond about 5 degrees, so f is large enough to allow rotation
3. Weak vertical wind shear, so the warm core is not ventilated away
4. A pre-existing disturbance to organise the convection, usually an easterly wave
5. Deep conditional instability and mid-level moisture, so entrainment does not kill the updrafts

**Inhibitors.** Strong shear, dry mid-level air (the Saharan Air Layer), cool SSTs or upwelling in the storm's own wake, landfall, and proximity to the equator.

## Exam 2022, Section A (answer one, 60 marks)

Sixty marks in about an hour. That is a real essay: plan it for five minutes, use headings, and put a labelled diagram on roughly every page. The instruction on the paper is explicit that a brain-dump scores badly, so answer the question that was asked and be selective.

### (a) Components of the climate system and the role of water

Skeleton: the five components (atmosphere, hydrosphere, cryosphere, lithosphere and land surface, biosphere), each with its characteristic response time, which is what makes the coupled system interesting. Then the global energy balance: incoming solar 100 units, planetary albedo 30, absorbed 70, and 70 of outgoing longwave, with the surface emitting far more than it absorbs and the difference closed by downward longwave. Use the Q7 (b) numbers as your quantitative spine.

Then water, phase by phase, which is what the question is really testing:

- **Vapour**: the strongest greenhouse gas, and a **feedback** rather than a forcing, because its concentration is set by temperature through Clausius-Clapeyron at about 7 percent per K. Roughly doubles the sensitivity to CO2.
- **Liquid**: clouds, which both raise albedo (cooling, dominant for low cloud) and trap longwave (warming, dominant for high thin cloud). The net is a small cooling with the largest uncertainty in the whole budget. Also the ocean, which is the heat reservoir and the thermal inertia of the system.
- **Solid**: ice and snow, the highest albedos on the planet, the ice-albedo feedback, and the latent heat of fusion buffering polar temperatures.
- **The transports**: 24 units of the surface energy budget leave as latent heat, so evaporation and condensation are how the surface exports most of its non-radiative energy, and the poleward moisture flux carries a large share of the meridional heat transport.

### (b) The atmosphere and habitability

Two halves, and the question tells you both. First, why the atmosphere makes Earth habitable at all: the greenhouse effect lifting the mean surface temperature from about 255 K to about 288 K, the ozone shield, the liquid-water pressure floor, oxygen, and the ocean and atmosphere circulations that move heat poleward. Then the counterfactual the question hands you: if the atmosphere were transparent to infrared, the surface would radiate straight to space, the 98 units of downward longwave would vanish, and the surface would sit near the effective temperature of 255 K, so frozen. Work that through the numbers rather than asserting it.

Second half, why some places are more habitable than others: the latitudinal insolation gradient, the Hadley cell putting deserts at 20 to 30 degrees, continentality against maritime moderation, orography and rain shadows, cold and warm boundary currents (the Benguela and the Agulhas are the local example and they explain why South Africa's two coasts differ so much), and altitude.

### (c) Shortcomings and advantages of GCMs

Formulation: discretised primitive equations on a rotating sphere, coupled atmosphere, ocean, land and ice components, typically 50 to 200 km horizontal grid, with everything below that parameterised.

Advantages: physically based rather than statistical, so they extrapolate to states never observed; internally consistent across all variables; support attribution by allowing counterfactual runs with a forcing removed; reproduce the observed 20th century; and give a global picture in one framework.

Shortcomings: coarse resolution misses convection, tropical cyclones, orography and coastlines; parameterisation is the largest source of spread, especially cloud and convection; systematic biases (double ITCZ, Southern Ocean cloud and radiation errors); drift and the need for spin-up; internal variability means a single run is one realisation, so you need an ensemble; scenario uncertainty is not model uncertainty; and computational cost limits both resolution and ensemble size.

Regional prediction: this is where the question is going. A GCM cannot resolve a 60 km sea breeze or the Cape Fold Mountains, so regional application needs downscaling, either dynamical (an RCM nested inside, which then inherits lateral boundary condition problems and the host model's biases) or statistical (cheap, but assumes the observed relationship survives into a changed climate). Say which you would use and why.

### (d) Is global warming real, and can we stop it

Define the terms first, because the question lists them and marks will be attached: radiative forcing (W/m2 imbalance imposed at the top of the atmosphere), climate sensitivity (equilibrium warming for doubled CO2, likely 2.5 to 4 K), feedback (the internal response that amplifies or damps, so water vapour and ice-albedo positive, Planck negative, cloud uncertain), variability (internal, returns) and change (forced, does not).

Evidence it is real: instrumental temperature records, ocean heat content (the largest and least ambiguous single number), sea level, cryosphere loss, the fingerprint pattern of stratospheric cooling alongside tropospheric warming, which greenhouse forcing predicts and solar forcing does not, and the isotopic signature of fossil carbon.

Then the three responses, each with advantages and disadvantages:

- **Mitigation.** Addresses the cause, is permanent, has co-benefits for air quality. Slow, needs global coordination, and warming already committed does not go away.
- **Carbon dioxide removal.** Directly reverses the forcing, and permanent if the storage is. But it is expensive, slow, land- and water-hungry at scale for biological approaches, and unproven at the gigatonne scale.
- **Solar radiation management.** Fast, cheap, and the only lever that can act on a decadal timescale. But it treats the symptom while ocean acidification continues, it carries a termination shock if stopped, it changes precipitation patterns unevenly so there are losers, and it raises governance problems no institution currently handles.

Take a position at the end, because the question asks which you would support and why. The defensible answer is mitigation first as the only approach that addresses the cause, CDR as a necessary complement for residual and hard-to-abate emissions, and SRM only as researched contingency and never as a substitute. Whatever you argue, argue it, and use the terms you defined.
