Answers to the six critical thinking questions for module 1, [[Week 1]] to [[Week 3]]. Questions set in `materials/Critical Thinking questions.docx`, applied to all module readings and the audio-visual. Prose first, then the evidence to fall back on if pushed. Cross-cutting material sits in [[Discussion prep]].
## 1. Controversies in the articles and the audio-visual
The main controversy is that Mabingo aims at people who believe they are the cure. His target is the wave of "African dance" programmes European and North American universities launched after 2020 as anti-racist pedagogy, and his charge is that the label they teach under is itself a colonial artefact, so the programmes reproduce the erasure they were built to fix. He is equally hard on Kariamu Welsh-Asante, an Afrocentric Black scholar, for her commonalities framing, which means the fault line is homogenising against specific rather than white scholarship against Black scholarship. A second controversy sits in the audio-visual. Adichie's single story is easy to accept, but the Lion King point is not, because the film is loved, employs people and still fixes Africa in a romanticised past. The uncomfortable version of the question is whether affection and harm can run through the same product, and whether our own course title belongs on the same list.
### Evidence
- The target is well-meaning anti-racist curriculum design, not open racism. That is what makes it uncomfortable: the people he criticises think they are the solution.
- He calls Evans-Pritchard's 1928 fieldwork what it was, research conducted under racist colonial rule of the Azande, and shows the slide from one Zande dance to "an African dance" happening in a single sentence.
- Sharpest move: he goes after **Welsh-Asante** for her commonalities in African dance (polyrhythm, polycentricity, curvilinear, dimensionality, holism, memory). Most of the room will miss that this is Black scholarship under attack from Black scholarship.
- Implicit charge of tokenism against institutions that add an "African dance" module and call the racism problem handled.
## 2. Areas of disagreement between the authors
Hanna 1973 is where the case is decided. She states the diversity herself, roughly a thousand languages and probably as many dance patterns, then writes "Nevertheless, I believe that it is possible to develop a general definition." She sees the problem, names it, and generalises anyway, and that single "nevertheless" is what Mabingo is arguing against. She also supplies the evidence against her own position: drums are central for the Ibo, Akan, Efik, Azande and Kamba, and absent for the Zulu, Matabele, Shi, Ngoni, Turkana and Wanyaturu, where drummers are sometimes despised. Welsh-Asante claims shared aesthetics across the continent, and Agawu 1995 answers that African rhythm is an invention and a myth. Mabingo sides with Agawu. Snipe and Hanna agree with each other on function, that the dance is utilitarian and inseparable from community need, while disagreeing with Mabingo on naming. Kuwor 2017 quietly settles it in practice: he keeps the label in his title and grounds every claim in Anlo-Ewe Ghana, which suggests the label is survivable when the content is specific.
### Evidence
Read Hanna directly before repeating Mabingo's version of her. She is more careful than his summary suggests, and that makes the argument better, not worse.
- Hanna is not the naive coloniser Mabingo's list implies. Hanna 1965 calls Africa "a continent diverse in indigenous culture and in the impact of modernity", and says Christian missionaries "with the cooperation of the imperial administrators, probably were most directly responsible for the modification, suppression" of dance. She was writing anti-colonial content under a homogenising title. Good counter-argument: the label is the problem, not the scholar's politics, which is actually Mabingo's own claim.
- Hanna 1973's drum example is the concrete counter to Welsh-Asante's commonalities. She undermines her own general definition with her own evidence.
- Welsh-Asante and Agawu 1995 contradict each other outright. No middle ground offered.
- Kuwor 2017 is the working counter-model: one people, one country, dance read through movement, music, multisensory modalities and visual form.
## 3. Most interesting aspects
The ballet argument is the strongest thing in the readings. Ballet was never filed under "European dance", so it accumulated vocabulary, notation and scholarly weight under its own name, and Mabingo's point is that homogenising labels stop knowledge from accumulating at all. Second is that the naming problem goes all the way down, since "Africa" is itself a Roman colonial term and Africans had marginal say in it, so the trouble did not start with dance. Third is the money. "African dance" is a commercial brand that sells because it signals exotic, and festivals, studios and media all have a stake in keeping it vague, which means the label can outlive the scholarship that refutes it. Fourth is Adichie's distinction that stereotypes are not untrue but incomplete, which is more useful than calling them lies because it explains why they are so hard to dislodge.
### Evidence
- "Africa" is a Roman colonial term of Berber or Greco-Latin origin (Mazrui 1986). The label problem does not start with dance.
- The ballet argument is Mabingo's closing move. Concrete and hard to rebut.
- The neoliberal hijack: follow the money and the label survives regardless of the scholarship.
- Adichie's incomplete-not-untrue distinction does a lot of work and is worth defending out loud.
## 4. The potential problem in the field
The practical bind. If we cannot say "African dance", we still have to name a course, a company, a festival and a degree, and Mabingo demands specificity without offering a naming framework for a programme that genuinely spans traditions. Ours is exactly that programme. There is also a floor to specificity, because "Zulu dance" is still one label across many communities, periods and functions, so the regress has to stop somewhere and nobody says where. Underneath both sits the harder problem: dance is transmitted through the body, so any label on a syllabus is already an abstraction of something that was never named for a catalogue. In practice the field also stays exposed to the market, which prefers the vague label and will keep supplying it whatever the scholarship decides.
### Evidence
- Mabingo demands specificity and offers no naming framework for a curriculum that genuinely spans traditions.
- Dance is transmitted through the body, not through text. Any syllabus label is already an abstraction.
- Specificity has a floor. Where does the regress stop?
## 5. What the articles overlook
Most of all, the dancers. Evans-Pritchard, Hanna and Cox write about African dancers and never with them, no practitioner is quoted by name explaining their own form, and Mabingo does the same thing while arguing against it, since his is a text criticising texts. That sits badly against this course's rule that African dance cannot be written from the outside. Gender is missing everywhere, although it decides who dances what and who may not, and sexuality is missing from the writing while present in the societies. Diaspora goes untouched, so we are not told what happens to Afro-Cuban, Afro-Brazilian or Black American vernacular forms if the label is invalid. African agency in the market is skipped, because African states, tourist boards and companies sell the same label deliberately. Authenticity is never defined, so "traditional" ends up meaning whatever the paying audience recognises. Method is unstated too, with no fieldwork length, working language or translator named, which leaves the claims impossible to check.
### Evidence
Worked through term by term in [[Discussion prep]]. Short version:
- **No dancers.** A text critiquing texts, against a course that teaches embodiment.
- **No alternative.** Mabingo says use Indigenous names, never says what to do when a programme covers ten traditions.
- **Gender and sexuality absent**, which is the gap Rani raised in lecture 1.
- **Diaspora absent.** Afro-Cuban, Afro-Brazilian and Black American forms go untouched.
- **African agency in the market absent.** He blames Western appetite only.
- **Authenticity undefined**, so the market decides it.
## 6. Contribution to dance education
They force curriculum writers to name the actual thing, Zulu, Ewe, Zande, Gumboot, Anlo-Ewe, rather than the continent. They reframe anti-racist pedagogy as something that can misfire while entirely sincere, which is a warning teachers need and rarely get. They give students a working vocabulary, alterity, invention of Africa, single story, coloniality, tokenism, commodification, that transfers to any field where a label flattens a people. Kuwor models the method the critique implies, one people studied through movement, music and multisensory practice at once. And they set up the rest of this semester, since colonisation and township modernity, appropriation, the tourist gaze and the black dance debate are the same argument applied to South Africa.
### Evidence
- Forces curriculum writers to name the actual thing.
- Reframes sincere anti-racist pedagogy as something that can still misfire.
- Hands students a transferable vocabulary.
- Sets up modules 2 to 5, [[Week 4]] onwards, all the same argument applied to South Africa.
