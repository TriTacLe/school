Cross-cutting prep for module 1, [[Week 1]] to [[Week 3]]. The eight terms Rani set, worked through with evidence, plus lines to quote and questions to put to the room. Question-by-question answers live in [[Critical thinking answers]].
## The article in one line
Universities in Europe and North America launched "African dance" programmes after 2020 as anti-racist pedagogy. Mabingo argues the label itself is a colonial artefact, so keeping it reproduces the erasure the programmes claim to fight. One word for 54 countries, over a billion people, thousands of traditions.
## The eight terms
They overlap, so keep the distinction sharp: gaps and absences are what is not in the text, alienation and marginalisation are what the text does to people, eurocentricity and bias are the lens, limitations and lacking depth are the method.
### Gaps
Questions the field never asks.
- **Authenticity is never defined.** Hanna's title "Africa's new traditional dance" only works if an old pure tradition existed. No reading says what that baseline was, when, or who certified it. Traditions were already trading and borrowing before Europeans arrived to record them.
- **What happens when dance leaves its context.** Snipe and Hanna both say the dance is utilitarian, art for life's sake. Neither asks what it becomes once lifted out of that life and staged for strangers.
- **Who profits.** No paper follows the money. Mabingo blames Western appetite and skips African states, tourist boards and companies selling the same label.
- Consequence: undefined "authentic" gets decided by whoever pays, and the test becomes whether it looks the way audiences expect Africa to look.
### Absences
People who are not in the text.
- **The dancers.** Evans-Pritchard, Hanna and Cox write about African dancers, never with them. No practitioner is quoted by name explaining their own form.
- **Individuals.** Everyone is a culture, nobody is a person. No biographies, no disagreements between practitioners.
- **Gender and sexuality.** Gender structures who dances what and who may not, and no author treats it. Homosexuality is absent from the writing while present in the societies.
- **Urban Africa.** Village, ritual and community appear. Township, studio, club and stage barely do, so the continent reads as static.
- Sharpest point for the room: **Mabingo has the same absence he attacks.** Texts criticising texts, no dancers. Against this course's embodiment rule, he is inside the argument and outside the practice.
### Alienation
The dancers are separated from their own practice.
- The observer holds the definition, the dancer supplies raw material. Africans are the subject of the sentence, never its author.
- Naming goes all the way down. "Africa" is a Roman colonial term (Mazrui 1986), so the people named had no hand in the name.
- Hall 1991: how people are represented is how they are treated. Represented as a single undifferentiated mass, then treated as one.
- Consequence: a Zulu or Ewe dancer meets their own form abroad rebranded as generic "African dance" and no longer recognises the description.
### Marginalisation
The form is pushed to the edge of the discipline.
- **The ballet argument**, Mabingo's closing move. Ballet was never filed under "European dance", so it accumulated vocabulary, notation and scholarly legitimacy under its own name. Lumped forms cannot accumulate, and every new writer restarts at "African dance is communal and rhythmic".
- Curriculum tokenism: one "African dance" module added, racism problem declared handled, colonial container kept.
- Kuwor 2017 shows the alternative. One people, one country, claims grounded in Ghana. Same subject, opposite standing.
### Eurocentricity
Europe as the measuring stick.
- Hanna 1973 p 165: early European observers "did not consider African dance to be dance, for it was not the classical ballet or foot-tapping folk dance of their home countries." The definition of dance itself was European.
- Evans-Pritchard 1928 p 446: "quite different from modern European dancing." The comparison is always to Europe, never Ewe to Zulu.
- Cox 2000 is a European reading of a Black American scholar reading African dance. Three removes from the studio floor.
- Mabingo's own frame: Ogude's "ultimate other", Mudimbe's invention of Africa. Africa exists in the literature as Europe's opposite, not on its own terms.
### Bias
The authors' position bending the claim.
- Two opposite verdicts on the same dances, quoted by Hanna 1973: "the quintessential aesthetic" and "the expression of moral turpitude". Ecstasy and disgust, both projections.
- Primitivism and over-romanticisation are the same reduction flipped. Lion King is the modern version, loved and still fixing Africa as timeless.
- Afrocentric bias counts too. Mabingo attacks Welsh-Asante, a Black scholar, as hard as the colonial anthropologists. So the fault line is homogenising versus specific, not white versus Black. Most of the room will miss this.
- Evans-Pritchard's fieldwork ran under colonial rule of the Azande. Access itself was a product of power.
### Limitations
What the method could not deliver.
- Fieldwork length, working language and translators go unstated, so no claim can be checked. Research that cannot be falsified cannot self-correct.
- Sample slides to conclusion in one sentence: one Zande dance becomes "an African dance".
- Hanna 1973 supplies evidence against her own general definition. Ibo, Akan, Efik, Azande and Kamba dance with drums. Zulu, Matabele, Shi, Ngoni, Turkana and Wanyaturu do not, and "sometimes the users of drums are despised".
- Mabingo's limitation is different: he demands specificity and offers no naming framework for a programme covering ten traditions, which is exactly our situation.
### Lacking depth
Breadth bought at the cost of depth.
- Hanna surveys eleven or more peoples in a few pages. Nobody can be embedded in eleven cultures, so the depth comes from other people's ethnographies rather than from the dancers.
- The generalisation is deliberate, not accidental. Hanna 1973 p 165 states the diversity, then writes "Nevertheless, I believe that it is possible to develop a general definition." That "nevertheless" is the entire debate.
- Welsh-Asante's commonalities (polyrhythm, polycentricity, holism) smooth over the same difference from the Afrocentric side. Agawu 1995 answers flatly that African rhythm is "an invention, a construction, a fiction, a myth, ultimately a lie."
- Consequence: stereotype becomes curriculum. Adichie's incomplete story becomes the only story, then the syllabus, then the exam answer.
## Quotable lines
- Adichie 2009, quoted by Mabingo p 2: "The problem with stereotypes is not that they are untrue, but that they are incomplete. They make one story become the only story."
- Agawu 1995 p 387, quoted p 6: "'African rhythm,' then, is an invention, a construction, a fiction, a myth, ultimately a lie."
- Evans-Pritchard 1928 p 446, quoted p 6: "A short analysis of an African dance will show that its structure is quite different from modern European dancing." Written about one Zande dance.
- Ogude 2012 pp 13-14, quoted p 5: "Africa in the Western imagination has always been represented as the ultimate other, the repository of emptiness and the domain of untranslatable cultures."
- Ndlovu-Gatsheni 2015 p 214, quoted p 5: colonial imperatives "included map-making, the partitioning of Africa, domination, and promotion of a singular idea of an African people who were collectively thought of as inferior, backward and primitive beings."
- Mbembe 2002 p 246, quoted p 4: the black body "was supposed not to contain any sort of consciousness and to have none of the characteristics of reason or beauty."
- Mabingo p 9: "Dance forms such as ballet have grown into a profound body of knowledge and gained scholarly legitimacy because they were never marginalized under homogenizing categories such as European dance."
- Hall 1991, in the abstract: how people are represented is how they are treated.
- Hanna 1973 p 165, the sentence to bring: "Africa has about 1000 different languages and probably as [many dance] patterns. Dance styles vary enormously and so do definitions... Nevertheless, I believe that it is possible to develop a general definition."
- Hanna 1973 p 165 on early European observers: they "did not consider African dance to be dance, for it was not the classical ballet or foot-tapping folk dance of their home countries." The two verdicts she quotes are "the quintessential aesthetic" and "the expression of moral turpitude", ecstasy and horror at the same dances.
- Hanna 1973 p 166: "Until recently African dance was rarely art for its own sake. Rather it was utilitarian, 'art' for life's sake."
- Hanna 1965 p 13: "African dance is a dramatic microcosm of many of the cultures of which it is a part." Note the hedge "many of the cultures" sitting under a continental title.
## Terms to use
Alterity and the invention of Africa (Mudimbe 1988), single story (Adichie 2009), orientalism (Said 1978), coloniality, tokenism, neoliberal commodification, exoticisation of the Black body, homogenisation, labelism. Rani's eight subheadings for these weeks: gaps, absences, alienation, marginalisation, Eurocentricity, bias, limitations, lacking depth.
## Questions to put to the room
1. This course is called African Dance 1 and teaches Intsika, a technique built by pulling movements from across the continent and the diaspora. Is that the synthesis Mabingo warns about, or does it escape because it is authored, named and made from inside? Safe to ask, Rani already teaches "say Zulu dance, not African dance". See [[Practical - Intsika]].
2. Mabingo attacks Welsh-Asante as hard as he attacks the colonial anthropologists. Does that mean the problem is homogenisation itself rather than who is doing it?
3. If we drop the label, what do we call the degree? He never answers this.
4. Ballet kept its own name and grew. But ballet also had state patronage and written notation. Is the label really the causal factor, or is Mabingo mistaking a symptom for a cause?
5. Where does specificity stop? Zulu dance still covers many communities and centuries.
6. Who profits from keeping the label, inside Africa as well as outside?
