##### What did dance play in anti colonial resistance
- **dancing at all was the resistance.** colonialists banned dances for their own convenience, because the drumming disturbed european sleep and pulled workers away from european-controlled jobs, so continuing to dance was direct refusal (hanna 1973 p 167).
- **survival under suppression.** african dance "tenaciously persists as a vital force in spite of attempts to destroy or transform it" (hanna 1973 p 166). missionaries and administrators modified and suppressed it, and it outlived them.
- **it kept the knowledge the colonisers tried to replace.** anlo-ewe history, including the escape from the oppressive ruler agorkorli at notsie, was stored in movement, song and ritual because western documentation was not available. dance was the archive (kuwor 2017 p 49).
- **safe channel for criticism.** dances ridicule offenders in gesture and song, and masquerade or possession frees the dancer from libel or retribution. a protected way to say what cannot be said openly (hanna 1973 pp 167-168).
- **absorbing and mocking the coloniser's forms.** foreign elements are not passively accepted. ubakala girls satirise the western ballroom hold by exaggerating it in play; ubakala nwagbogho does the same (hanna 1973 p 171, hanna 1965 p 20).
- **protest inside entertainment dance.** west african entertainment dances took on the sacred and ceremonial and became "voices of social protest and cultural renewal" under colonial and post-colonial pressure (begho, in cox 2000 pp 109-110).
- **religion as the site of the fight.** ajayi frames it as a contest between indigenous religion and the two missionary religions. christianity arrived "anti-body" and therefore "anti-dance", and dance survived by contextualising inside new african churches such as the aladura (cox 2000 p 110).
- **carrying the political message.** kikuyu war dances were the medium for announcing the laws of a new government, and after kenyatta the war dances of all ethnic groups celebrated independence (hanna 1973 p 171).
- **nationalism and the state's use of it.** post-independence leaders revived dance to reclaim the status europeans denied it, funded national companies, and used dance-songs as party propaganda. also banned dances carrying antigovernment messages, so the same tool cuts both ways (hanna 1965 pp 15-17, hanna 1973 p 167).
- **cultural nationalism as policy.** nkrumah opened the institute of african studies in 1963 to study african arts "in new african centred ways", making dance research part of the decolonisation programme (kuwor 2017 p 47).
- **naming as the current front.** decolonising continues in what the dances are called. indigenous names are the starting point, not the homogenising label (mabingo 2022 p 9).

##### Why is there none info about SA
- **because these articles are about independence, and sa had none.** hanna writes on *new traditional* dance in newly independent states: national companies, state funding, war dances celebrating independence after kenyatta (hanna 1965 pp 15-17, hanna 1973 p 171). in 1965 and 1973 sa was under apartheid, so it had no place in that story. my reading, not hanna's.
- **and because one region is made to speak for the continent.** the readings are nigeria, uganda, ghana. that is the single story: stereotypes are "incomplete", not untrue (mabingo 2022 p 2).
- **the fieldwork followed the colonial map.** evans-pritchard analysed one zande dance while working under "the RACIST colonial rule of the Azande people" (mabingo 2022 p 6). the discipline went where colonial and then anglophone access ran, so it wrote the places it could enter.
- **nobody in the module writes from inside sa.** kuwor writes anlo-ewe because he is anlo-ewe, from a named community (kuwor 2017 pp 47-48). same point as the body-as-knowledge question above: the missing material is the material lived rather than observed.
- **the venues are western.** ethnomusicology, yearbook of the ifmc, dance research. sa work of that period was not what these journals commissioned. my reading, not stated in the articles.
- **so this is an absence, not a gap.** it was never written here, not misplaced. two of rani's eight terms, and this question is the difference between them.

##### What is African enough
- **the readings say the question itself is the problem.** "african" as a category was named from outside, a roman term for a province in tunisia, so africans "may have had marginal agency in the naming of the continent on which they lived" (mazrui, in mabingo 2022 pp 3-4). an authenticity test built on that word tests you against the coloniser's category.
- **the usual answer is a checklist, and it fails.** welsh-asante's commonalities, polyrhythm, polycentricity, curvilinear, holistic, offer a continent-wide aesthetic (cox 2000 p 110). mabingo says the dances "are more complex and diverse" than that, and quotes agawu: "'african rhythm', then, is an invention, a construction, a fiction, a myth, ultimately a lie" (mabingo 2022 p 6).
- **kuwor's answer: belonging, not purity.** africa's cultures differ and still "produce commonalities that provide some sense of belongingness", and any ghanaian culture is an african culture (kuwor 2017 p 48). it is african because it is a people's, not because it passes a test. his condition is that you situate it in its proper context.
- **hanna's answer: change does not disqualify.** the argument of both articles is continuity of change. dance "tenaciously persists as a vital force" while being transformed (hanna 1973 p 166), and ubakala girls satirising the western ballroom hold is still ubakala dance (p 171). "african enough" as frozen and pre-colonial is a category the literature itself does not support.
- **so: wrong question, and worth asking who is asking it.** the working answer is mabingo's, name the dance, the people, the occasion (p 9). then "african enough" has nothing to grip.


##### Why didnt the articals talk about gender stuff
- **gender roles: they wrote about them constantly, they just never made gender the question.** hanna 1965 has zikbo danced by ubakala clan women, and women splitting by political party and refusing to dance together, one group singing insults about the democratic party while the male musicians played (p 17), plus acholi girls' costume changing at puberty and the clothing history of baganda women after the british arrived (p 18). women are the material, never the analysis.
- **where hanna gets closest, she reads role as function, not power.** lotuka girls dance seriously because prospective husbands are watching (p 170), ubakala girls rehearse heterosexual contact through nwagbogho (hanna 1973 p 171, hanna 1965 p 20), women crying and speaking in tongues to vent repressed feelings from separation (p 169). dance teaches girls their coming role, and that arrangement is described as working rather than questioned.
- **lgbtq and trans: zero. searched all five, not one mention.** the only sexuality in the readings is heterosexual courtship and marriage. the frame is the community reproducing itself, so anything outside that has nothing to attach to.
- **mabingo names gender once and drops it.** quoting hall, the continent is cut by "nationality, ethnicity, class, gender, and age" (mabingo 2022 p 7). he builds nothing on it. an essay about categories that flatten people leaves that one alone, which is his own single story.
- **kuwor is the only one who treats any of it as a problem.** the drum text warning that womanising ends in bankruptcy (pp 58-59), and the early twentieth century song about the schoolgirl made pregnant by her teacher, which he uses to name sexual abuse and female school dropout (pp 59-60).
- **why: these were not categories in this field yet.** 60s and 70s ethnography asked what dance does for the community, so the body appears as costume, role and courtship, not as a position in a power structure. same shape as the sa absence above. my reading, not stated in the articles.

##### What is your understanding of decolonizaion
- **it is not independence. the flags changed and the knowledge did not.** nkrumah opened the institute of african studies in 1963 to study african arts "in new african centred ways", and kuwor reads the coup and his exile as the moment africa went back to using imported knowledge to build itself (kuwor 2017 p 47). political freedom arrived, epistemic freedom did not.
- **it starts at the name.** indigenous dance names are the starting point, not the homogenising label, and ballet gained scholarly legitimacy "because [it was] never marginalized under homogenizing categories such as european dance" (mabingo 2022 p 9). name the dance, the people, the occasion.
- **it is a method problem, not only a content problem.** labanotation records the body's exterior and misses interior feeling and spirituality, so it can only give a partial picture (kuwor 2017 p 61). if the instrument cannot hold what matters, the record keeps confirming the coloniser's account. change the instrument, not just the topic.
- **the source moves to the body and the first person.** kuwor has danced agbadza for thirty years, quotes tradition keepers, translates what the drums say. that is the point from the body-as-knowledge question above: lived, not observed.
- **it is not going back to a frozen past.** dance "tenaciously persists as a vital force" while being transformed (hanna 1973 p 166). a pre-colonial purity test is the same trap as "african enough", just pointed the other way.
- **and it can be captured.** post-independence states funded national companies, used dance-songs as party propaganda, and banned dances carrying antigovernment messages (hanna 1965 pp 15-17, hanna 1973 p 167). mabingo diagnoses the label in english, in an american journal, inside the academy he indicts. decolonising is a claim anyone can make, so ask who benefits from the version being sold.
- working position for the room: decolonisation is a change in **who produces knowledge and by what method**, not a change in subject matter or in who holds office. rani has made work under this exact title, "decolonising the african dancing body" at the baxter, so it is worth asking him what he meant by it.
##### What does decolonisation of african dance look like in academic modern spaces
- **this is exactly what mabingo is writing about.** his target is the wave of "african dance" and "west african dance" programmes that opened in european and north american universities and studios after black lives matter (p 2). his charge is that building anti-racist curricula under that label extends the racism they claim to fight (pp 2-3). so step one inside a university is the course title.
- **rename the curriculum after the dance and the people.** start from indigenous dance names and engage communities inside africa on how programmes should be framed (mabingo 2022 pp 8-9). his test: ballet became "a profound body of knowledge and gained scholarly legitimacy because [it was] never marginalized under homogenizing categories such as european dance" (p 9).
- **watch what the institution gets out of it.** the label sells because it signals exotic, which "feeds the prevailing exoticization and commodification of the black body", the same "mining and refining" extraction that enriches the appropriating agent rather than the source community. departments that keep the label risk "institutionalized tokenism" (p 8). a module can be a box being ticked.
- **change what counts as evidence.** the academy privileges the written article, and labanotation records the exterior while missing interior feeling and spirituality (kuwor 2017 p 61). his alternative, greenotation, was built from long fieldwork with african practitioners, and the files and computers holding it are now "nowhere to be found" at the university of ghana (pp 61-62). the decolonial tool existed inside an african university and was lost to neglect. that is the whole problem in one example.
- **change who is in the room and where they stand.** kuwor writes as a thirty year agbadza dancer, quotes tradition keepers and translates drum text (pp 58-59). nkrumah's institute of african studies, 1963, was meant to study african arts "in new african centred ways" (p 47). african centred means the practitioner is a source, not a subject.
- **and hold the contradiction.** mabingo makes this argument in english, in an american journal, inside the academy he indicts. the same tension sits in a studio teaching intsika, a technique assembled from across the continent. worth putting to rani, since he named a piece "decolonising the african dancing body".
