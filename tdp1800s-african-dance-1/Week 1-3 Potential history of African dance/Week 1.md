## the homogenising label (mabingo 2022)
- central question: why "african dance" and not zulu dance, ewe dance, or at least east, west, north, southern african dance? one label flattens 54 countries, over a billion people and thousands of dance traditions into a single monolith.
- the label's genealogy is colonial. mudimbe (1988) calls it the "invention of africa": europe framed indigenous embodied knowledge as primitive, devilish, backward, then remade africa through religion, law, commerce and education. even the name "africa" is a roman colonial term of berber or greco-latin origin (mazrui), not chosen by the people it names.
- adichie's "danger of a single story": stereotypes are not untrue but incomplete; they make one story become the only story.
- irony mabingo highlights: western universities now launch "african dance" courses as anti-racist pedagogy, but by keeping the homogenising label they reproduce the same colonial erasure they claim to fight.
- even sympathetic scholarship did this: evans-pritchard (1928) labelled one zande dance "african dance"; hanna, segy, thompson and others carried the label forward; welsh-asante's "commonalities" framing (polyrhythm, polycentrism, holism) still smooths over difference.
- lecture takeaway: always specify which dance. "we are doing zulu dance", not "african dance". labelism reduces; specificity respects.
## misrepresentation and over-romanticisation
- outsiders write about africa as only indigenous and traditional, as if the continent does not evolve. example from lecture: the lion king. globally loved, commercially huge, feeds people, and still cements a romanticised, static image of africa. money for some, prejudice for the continent.
- african dance abroad is a business. export version gets marketed as "authentic", which loops back into stereotype.
- people misjudge africans as primitive; over-romanticisation is the flip side of the same reduction.
- politics shapes how african dance develops. sa was the last country on the continent to gain freedom (apartheid until 1994), still "looks like europe" in many ways, and has been labelled "not african enough" by other african scholars.
## embodiment
research on dance must be embodied and lived, not only read. you cannot write african dance from the outside.
## globalisation, fusion, coloniality
- globalisation: a form popular in its locality becomes globally understood. brings fusion, and narratives become fluid.
- coloniality: the power structures of colonialism persist in how dance is written about, taught and sold after formal colonialism ends.
## gender blindness
- african dance literature rarely treats gender, even though gender roles clearly structure the dances (who dances what, who cooks, who works).
- homosexuality in african dance is nearly absent from scholarship, though it exists in african societies. there are even songs celebrating violence against gay people.
- sa paradox: the only african country with legal same-sex marriage, yet lesbians are still killed in cape town townships. law permits what practice punishes.
- dance reflects the social behaviour of a people, so these silences in the literature are silences about the societies too.
## research example from the lecture
forced child marriage in sa villages: children married off to old men. art used to empower and educate around the issue. model for how dance research can address social problems, and a counter-example to how east and west write about africa.
