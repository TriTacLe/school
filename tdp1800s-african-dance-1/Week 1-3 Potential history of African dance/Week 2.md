## who writes about african dance
- rani's figure from the room: **84% of books about african dance are written outside africa.** his question was why, not whether. treat the number as his, and find a source before it goes anywhere near the research paper.
- what is represented in papers about africa is not always drawn from reliable sources. secondhand ethnography gets recycled as fact, and each retelling loses the conditions it was collected under.
- the question of neutrality. a paper that presents itself as neutral is still written from somewhere, and the field has treated the outsider position as the neutral one.
- from the readings: this is the absence worked through in [[discussion prep#absences]]. evans-pritchard, hanna and cox write about african dancers, never with them, and no practitioner is quoted by name explaining their own form.
## african research on its own terms
the strongest thread in the lecture, and the one my notes are thinnest on.
- africa is still researched in a way that is distinct from how africa would research itself. the method arrived with the people who wrote the first accounts and it stayed.
- the challenge is to write about ourselves and build our own structure for doing it, rather than borrowing a framework that was designed to look at us from outside.
- rani's comparison: **china and japan do not compromise.** they write on their own terms and accept being excluded from western validation for it. same in the middle east. the implied question for us is whether african scholarship is willing to pay that price, or whether it keeps writing for a western readership and keeps the categories that readership recognises.
- worth pushing on: this cuts against mabingo slightly. he diagnoses the label but argues inside the western journal system, in english, for an american education journal. good question for the room.
## homogenisation and what it erases
- "african dance" as a label is homogenising. africa is generalised as a whole without interest in the fact that it holds distinct parts, each with its own culture and differences, which cannot be collapsed into one.
- what gets erased specifically: distinct movement vocabularies, distinct spiritualities, distinct historical experiences.
- continental identity is privileged over local knowledge. the continent becomes the unit of analysis when the people, the language and the occasion are the units that actually carry the dance.
## eurocentricity
- the label problem and the eurocentricity problem are the same problem seen from two sides. one flattens africa into a single object, the other supplies the vantage point doing the flattening.
- from the readings: hanna 1973 p 165 on early european observers, they "did not consider african dance to be dance, for it was not the classical ballet or foot-tapping folk dance of their home countries." europe held the definition of dance itself.
## gaps and their consequences
- gaps were named again, and this time with the consequences attached rather than just listed.
- from the readings, the consequences worked through in [[discussion prep]]: knowledge stops accumulating, stereotype becomes curriculum, dancers lose the definition of their own practice, and research that states no method cannot be checked or corrected.

## power and the right to name
- the united states calls it soccer, everyone else calls it football. politics runs through the game and through the world cup.
- the point underneath: when you hold a particular kind of power you acquire the arrogance to reach into other people's lives and rename or rearrange them to suit yourself. that is dangerous for a society.
- ties straight back to the label. naming is not description, it is a claim of authority over the thing named.
## who produces the knowledge, and who reads it
- knowledge is collected by researchers, interpreted by researchers, published by researchers and read by researchers. a closed loop.
- it is written for a particular society, an elite group, not for the man in the street.
- the information itself is taken from the man in the street. he supplies the material and is then excluded from the result. that is the category of work being produced, and the label is part of the marginalisation that makes it possible.
- question rani put: african dance was written about in the 60s and 70s, so who was actually reading it back then? not the dancers.
- african dance papers do not carry an african voice, and people who are not from africa are the ones who like writing about african dance this way.
## silences that african scholars keep
- why are black people in africa afraid to write "black"? a marginalised voice, marginalised over the colour itself.
- abduction of african women and girls into marriage. why will black writers not take this on, and is the reluctance fear of the conversation? a large matter that gets ignored. rani's blunt version: why can we not arrest an adult who abducts a child to make her his wife?
- open question from the room, worth checking rather than assuming: where south african law actually stands on this. connects to the forced child marriage example from [[week 1]].
- also raised: is indigenous medicine better than western medicine? same structure of question, whose knowledge counts as knowledge.
## method, practice as research
- the practicals themselves should generate theory. theory does not have to arrive from outside the studio.
- **aurality**: knowledge carried in sound and in the oral, not only in text.
- **par, practice as research**: the practice is the research method, not an illustration of research done elsewhere. this is the methodological answer to the embodiment principle from [[week 1]], and it is the obvious route into the research paper.
## coloniality that outlived the colonisers
- taking what is not ours and making it ours. the mechanism did not stop when formal colonialism did.
- naming it colonialism is insufficient. coloniality is the better term, because the colonial power structure persists after the colonisers leave.
- missionaries labelled dancers as not spiritually acceptable, which is where a good deal of the suppression came from.
## romanticism and the rural stereotype
- romanticism about african dance, the rural side presented as the whole, africa framed as a place that does not evolve.
- reinforces the stereotype that african dance must be rural, historic and tribal.
- people who are not from africa are the ones who like writing it this way, and the writing then sells because that is the africa a foreign readership recognises.
## from cultural adaptation to political struggle
- dance has been discussed as cultural adaptation but not as political struggle. that has improved since.
- the better frame: cultural resistance, and post-colonial identity.
- identity only came into focus after **rhodes must fall**. rani's phrasing: the institution made room for black people but not for black things.
## the commonalities problem
- collective participation, communalism, shared african characteristics, all offered as what holds african identity together.
- they may hold in many contexts. they are not universally applicable, and that is the whole objection.
- this is welsh-asante's framing meeting its counter-example. see [[discussion prep#lacking depth]], where hanna's own drum evidence breaks the general definition she is arguing for.
## the eight terms, coverage
with these notes the lecture touches all eight of rani's eight module 1 terms. gaps and limitations from the first half. **absences** in the missing african voice and the ignored subjects. **alienation** in the man in the street who supplies the material and never reads the result. **marginalisation** named outright as what the label does. **eurocentricity** in who defines and who publishes. **bias** in the romanticism and the rural stereotype. **lacking depth** in commonalities that are asserted continent-wide and do not hold. worked through against the readings in [[discussion prep#the eight terms]].
