### Course description, condensed
- **Practical.** First of the technique and percussion courses at the Centre. Intsika, Rani's open-ended Pan African technique from 2002, developing mind, body and spirit together. Draws key movements from across the continent and the Diaspora, stylised for studio presentation, aimed at beginners and at dancers trained in another discipline. Covers West, East, Central and Southern African dances. Framed as old-to-new interpretation through "African dance pedagogy of pluralism".
- **Theory.** Past, present and future of African dance history. Defining and interrogating African dance, transition and continuity in 21st century spaces, appropriation and appreciation of Indigenous dances, African contemporary dance in South Africa and its socio-political conditions, local and global modernity and urbanism from traditional through neo-traditional to South African contemporary work. Explicitly framed around decolonisation and transformation, and around interrogating the scholars who documented the field across history, ethnography, ethnomusicology, gender, race and culture.
### Objectives worth remembering
- Practical: Intsika vocabulary, body alignment, rhythmic sensibility, energy flow, coordination, flexibility, dynamics, strength, longer combinations, studio protocols for the traditional rituals, percussion and song, and awareness of how social traditional dances are adapted to a Western studio.
- Theory: African dance history at academic level, writing vocabulary and research material read through decoloniality, harder research writing and reading, the field's jargon and protocols, and being physically present in dance transformation in South Africa rather than only reading about it.

### Module 2, weeks 4-6
Colonisation and township modernity. Key terms: colonisation, politics, modernity, Black people, ways of dancing in the township(s).
- Rani, M. X. (2012). Lost meaning, new traditions: shaping identity in the "new" South Africa. In S. Friedman (Ed.), *Post-apartheid dance: many bodies many voices many stories* (pp. 73-88). Cambridge Scholars.
- Rani, M. X. (2018). The impact of colonisation on the ability to make a meaning of 'Black' South African contemporary dance in the 21st century. *Journal of Pan African Studies*, 12(4), 311-326.
- Johnstone, K. (2012). Community in concert: transformation, development and community dance. In *Post-apartheid dance* (pp. 147-168).
- Amin, T. N. (2011). A terminology of difference: making the case for Black dance in the 21st century and beyond. *Journal of Pan African Studies*, 4(6), 7-15.

Two of these are Rani's own, so the critique of homogenising labels meets the person marking the paper. Amin's "terminology of difference" is the naming argument again, one module early, and it sets up module 5.
### Module 3, weeks 7-8
Appropriation and politics in the SA dance arena of the 90s. Key terms: dance as political tool, appropriation versus appreciation.
- Glasser, S. (1993). Appropriation and appreciation. CORD conference paper, 1-13.
- Glasser, S. (1991). Is dance political movement? *JASHM*, 6(3), 112-122.

Appropriation is the market side of the label argument. If "African dance" sells because it signals exotic, the question is who is allowed to sell it and on whose terms.
### Module 4, weeks 9-10
Socio-economic impact and tourism on post-apartheid dancing bodies. Key terms: tourist gaze, new African dance as contemporary.
- Friedman, S. (2010). Struggling to be heard above the sound of the vuvuzela. SDHS conference proceedings, 97-108.
- Friedman, S. (2012). The impact of the tourist gaze on the voice of South African contemporary dance. In *Post-apartheid dance* (pp. 89-107).
- Kodesh, H. (2006). Cultural cocktails: the choreography of Gregory Maqoma. *Critical Arts*, 20(2), 39-60. The outline runs this together with Maqoma's own reply, "A response: beyond ethnicity", same pages. Two texts, one entry.
- Loots, L. (2006). Post-colonial visitations. *Critical Arts*, 20(2), 89-101.
- Szymajda, J. (2023). Dance as memory machine: contemporary African dance as a decolonizing practice. Institute of Art, Polish Academy of Sciences, 1-25.
- Douglas, D., Sichel, A., Liadi, A. M., Noel, K., Danster, R., Cuvilas, A., & Linyekula, F. (2006). Under fire: defining a contemporary African dance aesthetic. *Critical Arts*, 20(2), 102-115.

The tourist gaze is the mechanism behind the undefined "authenticity" gap. Whoever pays decides what counts as authentic, and Friedman names the payer.
### Module 5, weeks 11-12
Racial demographics in SA dance politics, does "black dance" exist. Key terms: black dance, black narratives, storytelling.
- Craighead, C. (2006). 'Black dance': navigating the politics of 'black' in relation to 'the dance object' and the body as discourse. *Critical Arts*, 20(2), 16-33.
- Loots, L. (2006). Post-colonial visitations. *Critical Arts*, 20(2), 89-101. Listed in module 4 as well, read once.
- Mamdani, M. (2001). Beyond settler and native as political identities. *Comparative Studies in Society and History*, 651-664.
- Nwadingwe, C. E. (2012). Body, space, and technology: interrogating unconventionality in postmodern African dance theatre. *African Performance Review*, 6(2), 27-42.
- Francis, K. (2011). Theatre of struggle and transformation. *South African Theatre Journal*, 20(1), 102-127.
- Loots, L. (2010). The body as history and memory. *South African Theatre Journal*, 24(1), 105-124.

"Black dance" is the same homogenising label on a different axis, race instead of geography. Mabingo's argument should transfer directly, and if it does not, that is the interesting result.

### The Snipe citation is wrong
The outline cites "Snipe, T. (2000). African dance: bridges to humanity. Dance Research, 18(1)". *Dance Research* 18(1) Summer 2000 pp 108-111 is not Snipe. It is **James L. Cox's review** of Welsh-Asante's edited volume *African Dance: An Artistic, Historical and Philosophical Inquiry*, and the review discusses Snipe's chapter inside that book. Snipe's actual piece is a book chapter, not a journal article, so it is not on JSTOR at all. The Cox review is in `readings/` as the closest available substitute.

Two things follow. Worth raising with Rani, since it affects anyone trying to cite Snipe. And note the irony: the Snipe chapter sits inside the very Welsh-Asante volume Mabingo spends a page attacking.
## Research paper
- Main topic: How can South African contemporary dance-theatre embody collective crowd consciousness and social unrest through movement, space, and symbolic objects in The Swelling Crowd?
- Alternatives: individual agency vs collective identity in protest choreography; crowd psychology translated into embodied performance; labour struggles and social unrest post-apartheid; the coffee-cart as symbolic object; social justice movements vs unintended civilian harm.
- Format: 3000 words, 12 pt, double spaced, justified. Minimum 15 references, Harvard style. Plagiarism declaration form attached (get from School of Dance secretary).
- Structure: abstract (250-300 words with keywords), introduction with clear statement, cohesive body with subheadings, conclusion linking back to topic, recommendations.
- Marking wants: compare and contrast scholars then commit to one that fits your context, credible sources, critical analysis with multiple viewpoints.
- Kuwor's 2019 Roehampton PhD in `readings/` is the long version of his 2017 article. Use it here, not for weekly discussion.
