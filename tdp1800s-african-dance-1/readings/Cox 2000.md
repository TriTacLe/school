Cox, J. L. (2000). Review of *African dance: an artistic, historical and philosophical inquiry* edited by K. Welsh Asante. *Dance Research*, 18(1), 108-111. [[Cox 2000 - Review of Welsh-Asante, African Dance (discusses Snipe).pdf|PDF]]
This is the stand-in for the Snipe reading on the course outline. The outline cites "Snipe, T. (2000). African dance: bridges to humanity. Dance Research 18(1)", but those pages are Cox's four page review of the Welsh-Asante volume. Snipe's "African Dance: Bridges to Humanity" is a chapter inside that book, not a journal article, so it is not on JSTOR. Details in [[African Dance 1#The Snipe citation is wrong]].
## What the book is, as Cox describes it
Welsh Asante (ed.), *African Dance: An Artistic, Historical and Philosophical Inquiry*, 254 pp, Africa World Press, Trenton NJ, 1996. Cox's frame is religion: "It is widely held that religion in Africa cannot be separated from other aspects of life. One cannot read this book without reaching a similar conclusion" (p 108). The volume is organised in four parts, tradition itself, tradition and continuity, tradition transformed, tradition contextualised.
## The chapters he singles out
- **Pearl Primus**, to whom the book is dedicated, describes African dance as so "hypnotic" that it draws the dancer "nearer and nearer to the centre of 'All-Being'", and argues that anyone wanting to understand African dance must also learn African religions (p 108).
- **Doris Green**: dance imitates all aspects of life, eating, pounding corn, hunting, fishing, self-defence, sexual activity, and is bound to life cycle rituals, especially puberty (p 108).
- **Kinni-Olusanyin**: the drum at the centre of ritual dance forms "a complete expression of symmetrical, consonant concert of sound and animated movement, colour and form" (p 108).
- **Robert Nicholls** opens "Tradition and Continuity" by calling indigenous cultures "functional social instruments" that learned to cope with extreme environments, which is why dance attaches to planting, harvest, new year and rites of passage (pp 108-109).
- **Tracy Snipe, "African Dance: Bridges to Humanity"** (p 109). Cox's summary: Snipe agrees with Nicholls that art in Africa cannot be separated from community need and adaptation to environment, then adds an analysis of dance and religion in the New World, tracing transcultural influence of African ritual dance in Voodoo in the United States, in Catholic Church rituals in South America, and in Yoruba dances still performed in Cuba, Brazil and Trinidad. Mariani's short chapter backs this up for Brazil.
- **Hazzard-Gordon** dates the sacred to secular split in the Americas to the middle passage, "By the early eighteenth century the sacred-secular split was fairly well established with respect to dance", and argues that what mattered most for African dance in North America was not its religious roots but the slave experience and the "slave dances" that followed. **Cheryl Willis** carries this to dance story and tap as forms rooted in an "African aesthetic" (p 109).
- **Felix Begho** divides West African traditional dance into sacred, ceremonial and entertainment, and shows each modified by "the cultural clash of colonial and post-colonial society": Aladura spirit-type Independent Churches innovating in sacred dance, new African governments reviving ceremonial dance for cultural pride, entertainment dance absorbing social protest, night club and puppet theatre dances appearing alongside (pp 109-110).
- **Omofolabo Soyinka Ajayi** treats contextualisation as a contest between African indigenous religion and the two missionary religions that "invaded" Africa, Christianity and Islam. Christian missionaries brought a Western attitude Ajayi labels **"anti-body" and hence "anti-dance"**, which is what suppressed traditional forms. Islam adapted to African cultures in many ways, but its prohibition on human representation hit dance, drama and sculpture. Her conclusion is survival, including inside new forms of Christianity and contextualised Islamic practice (p 110).
- **Welsh Asante** closes with two Zimbabwean forms, Jerusarema and Muchongoyo: flat-footed gliding steps discharged from a crouch, performed "to a propulsive rhythm that gives it a swinging quality". She reads the gliding step as the human connection to the earth, which Africans regard as friendly and benevolent, so dance carries ethical content and not only aesthetic or sacred images (p 110).
- **Rex Nettleford's foreword**, quoted by Cox at the end: Africans have been characterised by Westerners as savage and heathenistic, an attitude that watching African dance appeared to confirm, and in-depth research is what dispels it (p 111).
## Key takeaways
- Read this as **the position Mabingo is attacking**, in friendly summary. Cox endorses the volume as "a thorough and convincing account of dance as an integral, persistent and dynamic cultural expression throughout Africa and among the African diaspora" (p 111). That continental claim is exactly the homogenisation [[Mabingo 2022]] targets, and the book is the one where Welsh-Asante's commonalities framing sits.
- Ajayi's "anti-body, hence anti-dance" is the sharpest phrase in the review for the colonisation question, and it names both missionary religions rather than Christianity alone.
- Begho gives a usable three-part scheme, sacred, ceremonial, entertainment, plus what colonial and post-colonial pressure did to each.
- The diaspora material, Snipe on Voodoo and Yoruba survivals, Hazzard-Gordon on the middle passage, is the strongest reason to keep this reading despite the citation mess.
- Watch the limit: it is a review, four pages, one reader's summary of eleven other people's chapters. Cite Cox for Cox's judgement, not as a source for what Snipe or Ajayi actually argue. If Snipe matters to the research paper, get the book.
