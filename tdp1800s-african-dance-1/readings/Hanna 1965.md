Hanna, J. L. (1965). Africa's new traditional dance. *Ethnomusicology*, 9(1), 13-21. [[Hanna 1965 - Africas new traditional dance.pdf|PDF]]
First report on her 1963 fieldwork in English-speaking East and West Africa, done as a research associate at Michigan State and a visiting associate at Ibadan. Field kit was a 16mm Bell and Howell camera, a Robot 35mm sequence camera and a tape recorder (footnote 1, p 21).
## The argument in one line
Traditional dance across Africa has lost its old position because the religious and socio-economic functions it served have changed, and what has replaced it is a "new traditional dance": the same movement material carried on outside its original context, now profane, theatrical and recreational rather than sacred and communal (p 15).
## How the article is built
She sets out forces of change, then works through four aspects: purpose and occasion, costume, accompaniment, form of dance.
### Forces of change (pp 13-15)
- Before European contact, change came from inside the culture. In this century it came from European occupation: Christianity, Western education, better communication and transport, new urban centres (p 13).
- **Missionaries did the most damage**, "with the cooperation of the imperial administrators", because dance was part of indigenous religion. Performance was read as "the manifestation of savage heathenism" and therefore hostile to the true faith (p 14).
- Europeans also passed secular moral judgment. Under Victorian morality, dancing with pelvic movement was branded licentious display, when it was often a glorification of fertility tied to harvest and survival. "African dances were, in other words, threatening to the European superego." That condemnation was then taught to Africans, "who learned to well, that most things African were inferior to their European counterparts" (p 14).
- Western education broke transmission physically: secondary schools were boarding, widely dispersed, with poor study facilities at home, so children were removed at exactly the age when dance is learned (p 14).
- Labour migration finished the job. Her example is a young Nigerian, the best dancer in his village, who taught the children every market day and then left to drive a taxi in Lagos, 400 miles away (p 14).
### Purpose and occasion (pp 16-17)
- Dance survives at marriages, chief installations, school and hospital dedications, graduations, Christian holidays, and after church services, so continuity runs through substitution rather than through preservation (p 16).
- For illiterate urban migrants, traditional dancing in town is recreation, belonging and a reaffirmation of clan identity in an alien city (p 16).
- Governments took it up: national companies in Guinea, Ethiopia, Ivory Coast, Senegal; the Institute of African Studies at Ghana under Albert Opoku; Uganda's first nationwide dance competition in 1963; Kenya's independence auditions (pp 16-17).
- Political capture is explicit. Party colours in costume, dance-songs as propaganda, and in Acholi District, Uganda, women from one village refusing to dance together because they belonged to opposing parties, so mediators had to be called before the dance could continue (pp 17-18).
### Costume, accompaniment, form (pp 18-20)
- Costume: Victorian propriety still dictates cover-up, and "traditional" dress today is often layered over Western clothes. New costume marks "civilized" status but rarely helps movement the way the older functional dress did (pp 18-19).
- Accompaniment: instruments die when apprenticeship dies. The dingidingi dance is named after a one-string fiddle, and is now usually played without one. Beer bottles, oil-cans and Western chairs replace traditional idiophones (p 19).
- Form: fusion produces new dances. Dingidingi is a girls' version of the older nanga; Nwagbogho in Ubakala satirises European ballroom position; highlife absorbs mambo, cha cha cha and twist into "traditional" drum interludes (pp 19-20).
- The "dance of deviance", a solo in rags performed by people the community calls "crazy", tolerated but never incorporated. She reads it as a new form produced by the psychological strain of Western intrusion (p 20).
## Key takeaways
- Suppression was not one act. Religion removed the reason for dancing, education removed the learners, migration removed the teachers, and morality reframed the movement as obscene.
- Continuity is real but partial: movement and general meaning survive while the original purpose is often lost (p 15).
- Her closing move is a research argument. "Large areas of ignorance exist in dance history, and especially in knowledge about African dance", and the speed of change makes recording urgent (p 21). Dance research is offered as a buffer against homogenisation into "the Western mold" (pp 20-21).
- She defines her own term in footnote 2: traditional dance is dance an ethnic group considers its cultural heritage and which was not created or introduced by most current participants (p 21).
## Quotable
- p 13: "African dance is a dramatic microcosm of many of the cultures of which it is a part."
- p 14: dance was taken "simply as the manifestation of savage heathenism and, therefore, antagonistic to the 'true faith'."
- p 14: it was the "white man's burden" to bring his civilization to the black man, and that "moral" civilization "had no room for the uninhibited and expressive dances of Africa".
- p 15: the new traditional dance "has changed from primarily a sacred, communal, ritualistic role to more of a profane, theatrical, and recreational one".
## Where it sits in the course
- Direct evidence for **colonisation and its effect on dance tradition**, with missionaries as the named mechanism.
- Supports **eurocentricity** and **bias**: Europe supplies the moral frame that judges the movement.
- Cuts against Mabingo's grouping of her with the label-users: her politics are anti-colonial while her title is continental. See [[Mabingo 2022]] and the counter-argument in [[Discussion prep]].
- Compare with [[Hanna 1973]], which reuses this fieldwork but argues continuity harder than loss.
