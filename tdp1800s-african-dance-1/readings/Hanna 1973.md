Hanna, J. L. (1973). African dance: the continuity of change. *Yearbook of the International Folk Music Council*, 5, 165-174. [[Hanna 1973 - African dance, the continuity of change.pdf|PDF]]
Based on the same 1963 fieldwork as [[Hanna 1965]] plus a decade of reading. Shorter, more theoretical, and the one that gets quoted against her.
## The argument in one line
Dance in Africa persists "in spite of attempts to destroy or transform it" because it does jobs a society needs done, and those jobs outlive any particular ritual context, so change is the mode of continuity rather than its opposite (p 166).
## How the article is built
### What is African dance (pp 165-166)
- Opens on European reception: African dance sent some Western artists "into ecstasy" and missionaries and colonialists "into a state of horror". Two verdicts on the same dances, "the quintessential aesthetic" and "the expression of moral turpitude" (p 165).
- Early European observers "did not consider African dance to be dance, for it was not the classical ballet or foot-tapping folk dance of their home countries" (p 165).
- **The passage the whole seminar turns on**: Africa has about 1000 languages and probably as many dance patterns, drums are central for the Ibo, Akan, Efik, Azande and Kamba, absent for the Zulu, Matabele, Shi, Ngoni, Turkana and Wanyaturu, where drummers are sometimes despised. Then: "Nevertheless, I believe that it is possible to develop a general definition" (p 165).
- Her definition: purposeful, intentional, rhythmical, culturally patterned sequences of nonverbal movement that are not ordinary motor activity, the motion having inherent value (p 165).
- "Until recently African dance was rarely art for its own sake. Rather it was utilitarian, 'art' for life's sake" (p 165).
- She then lists dance as physical, artistic, cultural, social, psychological, economic, political and communicative behaviour (pp 165-166).
### Forces of change (pp 166-167)
Same material as the 1965 article, compressed. Imposed religions destroyed the raison d'etre of much dance; missionaries backed by administrators were "probably most responsible for the modification, suppression, or disappearance of traditional dancing"; colonialists also banned dances for convenience, because drumming disturbed European sleep and drew workers away from European-controlled jobs (pp 166-167).
After independence, states revive dance for national identity, or permit only the dances that do not conflict with modernity programmes, or ban dances carrying antigovernment messages. She lists thirteen countries sending national companies abroad (p 167).
### The five functions (pp 167-170)
This is the analytic core, and it is openly functionalist (footnote 8 cites Talcott Parsons).
1. **Teaching and reinforcing cultural patterns.** Chaga shiganu dances back up oral instruction; dances ridicule offenders, with masquerade or possession giving freedom from retribution; Bavenda domba teaches sexual lore in initiation (pp 167-168).
2. **Managing tension.** Dogon masked dances at death; Ubakala girls' "Pregnant Child" dance rehearsing the fear of marriage and childbirth; Irigwe women dancing out the strain of repeated separation. Lambo's psychiatric claim that dance prevents depression and discharges psychic stress (pp 168-169).
3. **Attaining goals.** Dance as motivation for battle and for work, for example Nupe women pounding hut floors in concentric circles (p 169).
4. **Adaptation, especially to the supernatural.** Worship through bodily action, possession as a channel for messages, Kiganda parents of twins dancing in friends' gardens to transmit fertility to the crops, the Bamana Tyi Wara mask invoking the god of agriculture (pp 169-170).
5. **Integration.** Initiation, courtship, solidarity. Ubakala women in a circle singing "Peace be with you as we come together"; Dahomean rank read off restraint, where the chief's steps are deliberate and the leaps belong to lower rank (p 170).
### Continuity in new contexts (pp 170-172)
- War dances become history and national identity; Kikuyu war dances once announced a new constitution, and after Kenyatta they celebrate independence (p 171).
- Urban migrants use traditional dance for belonging and relief from a hostile city (p 171).
- Foreign elements are not passively accepted. Ubakala girls satirise the ballroom hold by exaggerating it in play (p 171).
- Dance enters churches, including where doctrine forbids it. Her Apostolic church example 20 miles from Ilesha reappears from the 1965 article (p 172).
- She concedes the loss: performing for tourists, festivals, national companies and staged choreography make dance "more 'artistic', profane, and professional, divorced from important aspects of individual and collective life" (p 172).
## Key takeaways
- The title is the thesis. Change does not interrupt continuity, it carries it, because the functions survive the context.
- **She supplies the evidence against her own generalisation.** The drum passage on p 165 is a counter-example to any continent-wide aesthetic, and she generalises anyway. That single "nevertheless" is the hinge Mabingo attacks.
- Her functionalism has a cost: every dance is explained by the need it meets, so choreographic invention, individual dancers and disagreement inside a community drop out of the account. No practitioner is named or quoted anywhere in the article.
- Colonisation appears here as a mechanism with two motives, religious and practical. The practical one, banning dances because drumming disturbed European sleep and work schedules, is the sharpest detail in the piece (p 167).

## Where it sits in the course
- The direct counterweight to any claim that colonisation simply broke the tradition. Use it against [[Hanna 1965]] as well, since the same author reads the same fieldwork as loss there and as continuity here.
- Best single source for **lacking depth**: the general definition asserted continent-wide over her own contrary evidence.
- Read next to [[Mabingo 2022]], which cites her six times in one line as a label-carrier, and [[Kuwor 2017]], who keeps the label but grounds every claim in one people.
