Kuwor, S. K. (2017). Understanding African dance in context: perspectives from Ghana. *Africology: The Journal of Pan African Studies*, 10(4), 47-64. [[Kuwor 2017 - Understanding African dance in context, Ghana.pdf|PDF]]
Kuwor is at the Department of Dance, University of Ghana, Legon, and has danced Agbadza for over three decades. The long version of this argument is his 2019 Roehampton thesis, [[Kuwor 2019 - Transmission of Anlo-Ewe dances (PhD thesis).pdf|PhD thesis]], which is for the research paper rather than for discussion.
## The argument in one line
Dance in Africa is a holistic form with four components, and Western analysis has consistently measured it with the wrong tools, so understanding it requires studying it in its own soil, from the inside, from a named community (pp 47-48).
## How the article is built
### The label question, answered head on (pp 47-48)
- He grants the postmodern objection: reducing a continent's forms to one genre "amounts to an open display of disrespect for the various ethnic groups" (p 48).
- Then he keeps the term anyway. "Once there is a European culture, Asian culture and American culture, there is African culture too." Africa is a continent of many countries whose cultures differ and still "produce commonalities that provide some sense of belongingness". Ghana alone has more than seventy local cultures, and any Ghanaian culture is an African culture (p 48).
- His condition is explicit: he uses "African dance" "with a high level of awareness that this may constitute generalisation if not situated in the proper context" (p 48).
- Nkrumah frames the politics. The 1963 speech opening the Institute of African Studies called for study of Africa "in new African centred ways", and Kuwor reads the coup and Nkrumah's exile as the moment Africa went back to using imported knowledge to build itself (p 47).
### The four components (p 48)
Movement, music, multisensory modalities, and visual forms (costume, shapes, designs, objects, patterns, colours). His claim is that Western accounts take component one and call it the dance.
### Agbadza as the worked example (pp 49-61)
- Anlo-Ewe settled in south-eastern Ghana around 1474 after escaping Notsie in present-day Togo, commemorated in the Hogbetsotso Za festival. With no written documentation, the escape and its lessons were stored in dance, song, ritual and names and passed down. Thirty-six communities hold Agbadza as collective identity (p 49).
- Etymology: agbe (life) plus dza (fresh or pure), so "fresh life" (p 49).
- **Movement in three segments** (pp 49-50):
  - *Preparation*, soft side steps with opening and closing arms. Read as instruction: any real move in life needs planning and resource mobilisation first. Life begins in the spine, and so does the dance.
  - *Main motion*, arms at ninety degrees rotating with the shoulder blades, sharp pelvic movement, feet keeping time. Read as an exhibition of the pace at which you are progressing in life. He notes this matches what Welsh called polycentricism.
  - *Climax*, sway right, sway left, then a sharp bow. Read as the judge's obligation: chiefs and elders must serve both parties equally. Performing Agbadza is one criterion for installing a chief. "Those who do Agbadza without the climax are considered as not knowing the dance" (p 50).
- **Music is inseparable.** The Ewe word *wu* means dance, music and drum at once. Ensemble: gankogui (double bell), axatse (rattle), kagan, kidi, sogo, atimewu. The gankogui is called gakpevi, the forged iron carrying a child, the big low bell as mother and the small high bell as the child on her back (pp 50-52).
- **Drum language** (pp 58-59). The drums speak sentences, and he gives field examples with translations: at a funeral in Anloga, atimewu "This is the dance for the dead" answered by kidi "They are covering his eyes with sand"; at a festival in Whuti, a warning that loving too many women leads to bankruptcy; and a posture instruction, "shoot your buttocks backwards, bend slightly", which is both aesthetic correction and injury prevention.
- **Songs** carry social criticism. He quotes an early twentieth century song about a schoolgirl made pregnant by her teacher who says she will abort to keep her education, and uses it to name teacher abuse as a cause of female dropout (pp 59-60).
- **Multisensory modalities** (p 61). *Seselelame*, the Ewe sensory modalities, work inside the body: movement starts in the spine and distributes energy to heart, lungs, digestion and reproductive organs, and includes spirituality. In the diaspora he found the spiritual side gave "cultural freedom and reunion".
- **Notation fails.** Labanotation captures the body's exterior and misses the internal feeling and the spiritual layers, so it can only ever give a partial picture (p 61). Friedson is quoted on the beauty of Agbadza sitting in the shoulder blades, not the arms: novices move the arms, the arms should move because the blades come together (p 61).
### Greenotation (pp 61-62)
Doris Green's system is the only tool he considers compatible with African music and dance, because she built it from long fieldwork with African practitioners. Green notated the Dangbe puberty dance Tokoe in Ghana in 2002, and Kuwor witnessed it as a student. His complaint: the files and computers holding Greenotation are now nowhere to be found at the University of Ghana.
## Key takeaways
- **This is the emic answer to the module.** Where the 60s and 70s literature describes African dancers, Kuwor writes as one, names his community, quotes tradition keepers, and translates the drums.
- His position on the label is a genuine third option between Hanna and Mabingo: keep the word, refuse the vagueness, ground every claim in a named people. Useful when the room treats the argument as binary.
- Movement is documentation. Anlo-Ewe history was stored in dance because writing was not available, which makes the dance an archive rather than an illustration of one.
- The critique of Labanotation is a method argument: if your recording tool cannot hold interior sensation and spirituality, your record will keep confirming that African dance is only movement.
- Oral transmission is the vulnerability he keeps returning to, and his fix is documentation on African terms rather than preservation in Western notation.
