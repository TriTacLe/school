Mabingo, A. (2022). 'African dance': the dangers of a homogenizing label. *International Journal of Education & the Arts*, 23(SI 1.2). [[Mabingo- Problematising African Dance.pdf|PDF]]
Mabingo is at Makerere University in Uganda, raised in Mbuukiro on Lake Victoria, and has taught in the US, New Zealand and Jamaica. The only module reading written by an African scholar working from the continent. Page numbers below follow the PDF's own numbering, 1 to 13.
## The argument in one line
The label "African dance" shares its genealogy with the colonial invention of Africa, so using it to build anti-racist dance programmes in Western universities extends the racism those programmes claim to fight (pp 2-3).
## How the article is built
### Introduction (pp 2-3)
- Written against a concrete target: the wave of "African dance" and "West African dance" programmes that appeared in European and North American universities and studios after Black Lives Matter (p 2).
- Frame from Stuart Hall (1991): how people are represented is how they are treated (abstract, p 1).
- The label "diminishes the diversity of more than one billion people, 54 countries, and thousands of complex dances into a form of stereotypical marginalizing homogeneity" (p 2).
- Adichie's single story: "The problem with stereotypes is not that they are untrue, but that they are incomplete. They make one story become the only story" (p 2). He ties it to Mudimbe's alterity and Said's orientalism.
### European colonialism and the invention of a monolithic Africa (pp 3-5)
- Mazrui: even the name "Africa" is Berber or Greco-Roman, a Roman term for a province in present-day Tunisia and eastern Algeria, so Africans "may have had marginal agency in the naming of the continent on which they lived" (pp 3-4).
- The colonial project used Victorian morality to frame Indigenous embodied knowledge as "backward, devilish, and primitive", and erased local epistemologies "through systematic structures of religion, law, commerce, and education" (p 3).
- Mbembe on the Enlightenment: Hegel and Kant identified in the African sign something "indelible" separating it from all other human signs, focused on "the black body, which was supposed not to contain any sort of consciousness" (p 4).
- The homogenised Black body was useful. Sameness made cheap labour and mass evangelising possible (p 4).
### The dangers of a single story (pp 5-7)
- Davis, Carter and Koff (2021): "Non-Western", "Ethnic", "World", "Urban", "Folk", "Street", "Vernacular" all mark nonwhite forms against a white frame (p 5).
- **Evans-Pritchard 1928** is the founding example. He analysed one dance of the Zande people and titled the analysis "an African dance", while working under, in Mabingo's capitals, "the RACIST colonial rule of the Azande people" (p 6).
- The list carried forward: Segy, **Hanna (1965, 1966, 1968, 1973, 1977, 1978, 1989)**, Knight, Serwadda and Pantaleoni, Thompson, Hart. Each writes about specific traditions and still uses the general label (p 6).
- **Welsh-Asante** is attacked directly. Her "Commonalities in African Dance" offers polyrhythms, polycentricity, curvilinear, dimensionality, holistic and memory as continent-wide aesthetics, and Mabingo says the dances "are more complex and diverse than Welsh's homogenizing narrative would want the reader to believe" (p 6).
- African scholars back him: Agawu (1995), "'African rhythm', then, is an invention, a construction, a fiction, a myth, ultimately a lie"; Nannyonga-Tamusuza quoting Sanjally Jobarteh that each tribe has its own music (pp 6-7).
- Hall (1994): there is no unified African identity, since the continent is cut across by nationality, ethnicity, class, gender and age (p 7).
### The neoliberal hijack (pp 7-8)
- The label is commercially useful precisely because it sells exoticism, and it "feeds the prevailing exoticization and commodification of the Black body" (p 8).
- Condensing many dances into one label is "mining and refining", the same extraction pattern as other Africa-West trade, and it enriches the appropriating agent rather than the source community (p 8).
- Institutions that keep the label risk "institutionalized tokenism" (p 8).
### Towards meaningful anti-racist labelling (pp 8-9)
- Fix: engage communities inside Africa on how programmes should be framed, and start from Indigenous dance names.
- The closing comparison is the strongest line in the essay. Ballet "grew into a profound body of knowledge and gained scholarly legitimacy because [it was] never marginalized under homogenizing categories such as European dance" (p 9).
## Key takeaways
- The charge is not that the label is inaccurate but that it is **institutionally productive**: it makes tokenism, commodification and curricular box-ticking possible.
- The fault line is not white scholarship against Black scholarship. Welsh-Asante is an Afrocentric Black scholar and takes the hardest hit, so the split is homogenising against specific.
- Naming is the whole mechanism. Mudimbe's "invention of Africa" means Africa was constituted as an object of knowledge by the people who named it, and the dance label continues that work.
- Specificity is the practical demand: name the dance, the people, the occasion. Rani's lecture 1 version of this was "we are doing Zulu dance", not "African dance".
- Fair objection to bring to the room: he diagnoses the label while writing in English, in an American education journal, inside the same academy he indicts. Set that next to Rani's China and Japan comparison in [[Week 2]].
