## MOL3000 and MOL3100: MCQ answer keys, Dec 2024 and Dec 2025

Both papers in `materials/Exams with answer suggestions/` carry answer
suggestions for the essay part but stop dead at the multiple choice section. The
MCQs are worth 48 of 100 marks in 2024 and a comparable share in 2025, so that
is the half of the paper with no key at all. Filled in here, one line of reasoning
per question, plus the two essay answers in the 2025 paper that were left
unfinished.

Reasoning is checked against the lecture slides in `materials/` where the
question is course specific rather than general knowledge. Where a question is
genuinely ambiguous it is flagged rather than papered over.

### Exam 11.12.2024, MCQ 1 to 24

Two marks for correct, zero for wrong, no negative marking.

| # | answer | why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | A, missense | Single base substitutions changing one amino acid are far and away the most common disease mutation class. Frameshifts and repeat expansions are individually severe but rare. |
| 2 | B | Of all SNVs catalogued in the population, most are rare, because rare variants vastly outnumber common ones. Watch the switch in C and D: of the SNVs in *your own* genome, most are common. Two different denominators. |
| 3 | D, high glucose in urine | Glucose spills past the renal threshold and drags water with it. Osmotic diuresis, not kidney damage. The thirst is secondary to the fluid loss. |
| 4 | B, glucose binds hemoglobin | HbA1c is non-enzymatic glycation of the haemoglobin beta chain. It reports the average glucose over the roughly 120 day red cell lifespan. |
| 5 | C, observed substitution frequencies | Both matrices are empirical, counted from real alignments. PAM from closely related sequences extrapolated outward, BLOSUM from conserved blocks. Neither is derived from chemical properties, which rules out A and B. |
| 6 | B, lower E value | E is the expected number of hits this good by chance, so smaller is better. A is the classic trap: `+` marks a conservative substitution, identity is the letter itself. |
| 7 | B, about 40 | $q = 0.01$, so individuals carrying at least one mutant allele are $1 - p^2 = 1 - 0.99^2 = 0.0199$, and $0.0199 \times 2000 \approx 40$. Answering 20 means you counted only heterozygotes as $2pq \times 2000 / 2$, a halving error. |
| 8 | C, 55 percent of homozygotes | Penetrance is the fraction of people with the disease *genotype* who show the phenotype. For a recessive disorder the disease genotype is homozygous, so 45 percent penetrant means 55 percent of homozygotes stay unaffected. A is wrong because heterozygotes are not expected to be affected at all. |
| 9 | D, true negatives detected | Specificity $= \mathrm{TN}/(\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FP})$. C is sensitivity. |
| 10 | D, inducing secretion of sIgA | Indirect means acting through the host. Bacteriocins, lactic acid and receptor blocking are all the flora acting directly on the pathogen. Priming the host to secrete IgA is the one mediated by the immune system. |
| 11 | B, concentration over time | PK is what the body does to the drug, PD is what the drug does to the body. A, C and D are all PD. |
| 12 | B, accessory genome | Virulence factors and resistance genes ride on plasmids, transposons, integrons and phage, all accessory. The core genome is the housekeeping set shared by the species. |
| 13 | B, genome-wide association study | |
| 14 | A | Monozygotic twins share all their DNA, dizygotic about half. A gap between the two concordance rates is the genetic signal. D inverts the logic. |
| 15 | C, *LDLR*, *APOB*, *PCSK9* | The three classic FH genes. The distractors smuggle in *HTT* (Huntington), *FMR1* (fragile X) and *APOE* (a lipid gene, but associated with type III hyperlipoproteinemia and Alzheimer risk, not classic FH). |
| 16 | D, nuclear DNA | The mitochondrion runs on roughly 1500 nuclear-encoded proteins against 13 mtDNA-encoded ones, so most mitochondrial disease genes are nuclear. B and C both say "always", and a nuclear-encoded mitochondriopathy follows ordinary mendelian inheritance, so neither holds. |
| 17 | B, uncontrolled immune reactions | Immune related adverse events: colitis, thyroiditis, hepatitis, pneumonitis, in the worst case cytokine release. Hair loss and diarrhoea in A are the chemotherapy signature, which is the distractor. |
| 18 | B, chronic inflammation | HBV and HCV cause decades of hepatitis, with repeated cell death, regeneration and fibrosis. The mutation load accumulates through that cycle rather than through direct viral transformation. |
| 19 | C, TET | Writers add marks (DNMT adds methyl, acetyl transferase adds acetyl), erasers remove them. TET oxidises 5mC toward removal. Kinase is neither. |
| 20 | C, a change in the DNA sequence away from the normal | A mutation need not be in a gene and need not cause disease. A, B and D each smuggle in one of those two restrictions. |
| 21 | D, piRNAs | Piwi-interacting RNAs, germline, silence transposons. Same question appears again in the 2025 paper. |
| 22 | D, about 48 percent | Hardy-Weinberg: $2pq = 2 \times 0.6 \times 0.4 = 0.48$. |
| 23 | C, less NO and more oxygen radicals | Superoxide scavenges NO to form peroxynitrite, so the two move in opposite directions by the same chemistry. Any option where they move together is wrong on mechanism alone. |
| 24 | D, 6-methyladenosine | m6A is the most abundant internal mRNA modification. Pseudouridine is abundant in rRNA and tRNA, 5hmC is a DNA mark. |

### Exam 08.12.2025, MCQ 1 to 25

| # | answer | why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | about 98 percent | Protein-coding exons are roughly 1 to 2 percent of the human genome. |
| 2 | recruits methyl-binding proteins that compact chromatin | Methylation works mostly by reader proteins (MeCP2, MBD family) pulling in HDACs and repressive complexes. Direct steric blocking of polymerase happens but is not the main route, so the first option is the near miss. |
| 3 | neutralizing positive charge of lysine | Acetylation kills the positive charge that grips the negatively charged DNA backbone, loosening the nucleosome, and creates a bromodomain docking site. |
| 4 | mediated by DNA repair-dependent enzymatic processes | Active means TET oxidation followed by TDG and base excision repair, no replication needed. Passive is the dilution-over-replication route, which is what the first option describes. |
| 5 | 6 genotypes | Three homozygotes plus three heterozygotes: $n(n+1)/2 = 3 \times 4/2 = 6$. |
| 6 | higher than 5 percent | The mirror image of MCQ 2 in the 2024 paper. Most variants *in a given genome* are common, because common variants are the ones you are likely to have inherited. |
| 7 | transdifferentiation bypasses pluripotency and directly converts one differentiated cell type into another | Checked against the stem cell lecture, which defines the two separately: transdifferentiation goes straight between differentiated states, while direct reprogramming is a short-term conversion *toward* the pluripotent stage without reaching it, then out to the target lineage. That makes the second option a deliberate swap of the two definitions. |
| 8 | A, RT-PCR analysis | Molecular level means expression of maturity markers. Size and proliferation are morphological or growth readouts, karyotyping tests genome integrity. |
| 9 | B, stem cell derived and self-organizing in 3D | Self-organisation is the defining property. |
| 10 | D, bone marrow niche, regulated by endothelial cells | The perivascular niche. HSCs circulate in peripheral blood, but reside in marrow. |
| 11 | B, glucose binds hemoglobin | Same as 2024 MCQ 4. |
| 12 | C, plasma C-peptide | C-peptide is cleaved off proinsulin in a one to one ratio with endogenous insulin and is not present in injected insulin, so it measures what the patient's own beta cells still make. Measuring plasma insulin instead fails in exactly the patients who are being injected with it. |
| 13 | C, delta cells | Alpha glucagon, beta insulin, delta somatostatin, PP cells pancreatic polypeptide. |
| 14 | A, faster identification enabling earlier targeted therapy | Speed is the whole clinical argument for syndromic panels. B is false, panels cost more. |
| 15 | B, produced faster and adapted quickly | The platform argument: swap the sequence, keep the process. C is plainly false, the COVID mRNA vaccines needed cold chain. |
| 16 | B, higher inoculum and weaker immune response | Same pathogen, different outcome means the difference sits in the host and the dose. |
| 17 | C, on a mobile genetic element such as a plasmid | Co-location of virulence and resistance on one plasmid is why they are co-selected and co-transferred. |
| 18 | the selection against mutation in one allele is lower in tumor suppressor genes | Tumour suppressors are recessive at the cell level, so one inherited hit is silent and can be carried through generations, with the second hit acquired somatically (Knudson). Proto-oncogene mutations are dominant gain of function, so a germline copy is usually developmentally catastrophic. The last option states that as an absolute, which is too strong. |
| 19 | crosstalk between pathways and re-organization of the networks | Checked against the Molecular Oncology slides, which make exactly this point: signalling is networks rather than isolated pathways, so blocking one node gets routed around, and combination inhibitors work better but are more toxic. Gatekeeper mutations are real, and the T790M story is the famous one, but they are the answer to a narrower question about a single drug and target. |
| 20 | immune checkpoint therapy | It targets the T cell response, not the tumour cell cycle. Chemotherapy hits dividing cells by definition, RTK inhibition and PARP inhibition both bite hardest in proliferating cells (PARP inhibition needs replication for the synthetic lethality to fire). |
| 21 | a state of permanent cell cycle arrest | Permanent, and the cell stays alive and metabolically loud, which is the point: the senescence-associated secretory phenotype. Not death, which is the first distractor. |
| 22 | utilizes sequence profiles from a multiple alignment | PSI-BLAST iterates: build a position-specific scoring matrix from the hits, search again with it, so weakly similar but genuinely homologous sequences come into range. |
| 23 | piwi-interacting RNA | Same as 2024 MCQ 21. |
| 24 | binding specific ligands with high affinity through steric interactions | Aptamers are folded nucleic acids acting like antibodies, shape recognition. The other three options are miRNA, piRNA and ribozyme mechanisms. |
| 25 | intergenic lncRNA | Between genes is intergenic, lincRNA. The last option says lncDNA, which is not a thing. |

### The two unfinished essay answers in the 2025 paper

**Essay Q1, mRNA vaccine optimisation (4 marks).** The paper's answer covers the
principle but breaks off at "modifications to the bases (e.g. pseudouridine)".
The full list, split by which half of the question it answers:

Raising antigen production:
- Codon optimisation of the coding sequence.
- A cap-1 structure, that is a 5' cap with 2'-O-methylation, for efficient
  ribosome recruitment.
- 5' and 3' UTRs picked for stability and translation, typically taken from
  highly expressed genes such as alpha- or beta-globin.
- A long poly(A) tail.
- Lipid nanoparticle delivery, which both protects the mRNA from RNases and gets
  it across the membrane.

Preventing unwanted inflammatory responses:
- Replacing uridine with N1-methylpseudouridine. This is the key one, and it does
  double duty. Modified bases are poorly recognised by the innate sensors TLR7,
  TLR8 and RIG-I, so the interferon response that would otherwise shut down
  translation and destroy the transcript never fires. Less innate sensing means
  both less inflammation and more antigen, which is why the same modification
  appears in both halves of the answer.
- The 2'-O-methylation on the cap serves the same purpose, marking the RNA as
  self so RIG-I and MDA5 ignore it.
- Purification, typically HPLC or cellulose, to strip out double-stranded RNA
  by-products of in vitro transcription. dsRNA is a potent interferon trigger and
  is the main contaminant that drives reactogenicity.

**Essay Q2 B, production organisms (4 marks).** The paper prints the heading and
nothing under it. Two are enough for the marks, with advantages and disadvantages
for each:

*E. coli.* Fast, cheap, grows to high density, genetics are trivial, very high
yields. But it does no glycosylation and no complex disulphide bonding, often
dumps the product into inclusion bodies that then need denaturation and
refolding, and carries endotoxin (LPS) that must be removed to clinical limits.
Fine for insulin and small non-glycosylated proteins, wrong for antibodies.

*Mammalian cells, in practice CHO.* Human-like glycosylation, correct folding,
correct disulphide bonds, secretes the product into the medium so purification
starts easier. But slow doubling times, expensive media, lower yields per litre,
and a real risk of adventitious viral contamination that forces viral clearance
steps into the process. This is the standard host for monoclonal antibodies and
other glycoproteins.

Yeast such as *Pichia pastoris* is a reasonable third answer, sitting between the
two: cheap and secretes well and does glycosylate, but the glycans are
hypermannosylated and not human, which can make the product immunogenic.

The pattern the examiner is testing: the choice of host is driven by whether the
protein needs post-translational modification, and everything else is a cost and
speed tradeoff downstream of that.
